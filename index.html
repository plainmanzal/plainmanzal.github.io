How I work

What I bring to a research team

I’m at my best one-on-one

Interviews and small sessions are where people tell you what actually happens, not what should happen. I like those conversations, and people tend to open up in them.

I pick the method for the question

Surveys to see how common a problem is, interviews to learn why, behavioral data to see what people really do, and experiments when I need to know what caused what.

Findings should be easy to act on

I write up each issue with the screen, what happened, why it matters and a specific recommendation, so engineers can fix it without a meeting to decode it.

Lab rigor, product speed

Psychology training gave me hypotheses, controlled procedures and APA-style reporting. Product work taught me when a quick audit is enough to make the next decision.

Protecting participants

Case study 01 · Voce Social · 2024–2026

Helping student leaders stop starting from scratch

Voce Social is an early-stage platform that brings everything a campus club needs into one place. Over three years I helped find the problems worth solving, narrow what the first version should do, and test the product before beta.

My role

Product Management Intern, focused on user research and usability

Timeline

May 2024 – present, remote

Team

The founder, an engineering team, and two fellow Wellesley interns

Methods

Survey, user interviews, synthesis, usability audit, beta testing

15+ → 3feature ideas narrowed to a research-backed first version
4 rolesdefined as design targets, each with its own pain point
11 issueslogged with fixes in the pre-beta usability audit
Sep 2026platform live for beta with Wellesley clubs

The problem

Context

Clubs run on six tools and nobody has the full picture

Announcements go out in a group chat. Updates go to an email list. Events go on Instagram. Files sit in one person’s shared drive, and sign-ups live in a Google Form. Each tool works on its own.

The real cost shows up at the end of the year. When an e-board graduates, its files, contacts and club history often leave with it, and the next board starts over.

How clubs run today

Group chatsEmail listsInstagramShared drivesSign-up sheetsGoogle Forms
→

Voce Social: one hub

  • Club profile and member roles
  • Shared calendar and events
  • Club files and transition docs
  • Tasks and announcements
  • Collaboration across campuses

Research questions

  1. What breaks when a club’s leadership changes hands?
  2. Which problems are shared across schools, and which are specific to one role?
  3. Before launch, can a student use the product without help?

Approach

Methods

Why these methods

Each phase asked a different kind of question, so each used a different method.

Survey · Jul 2024

How common is it?

We needed to know whether problems with handoffs and communication were widespread before building anything. A short form reached officers at several schools in about a week.

Interviews · 2024

Why does it happen?

Survey answers showed that leaders struggled but not why. One-on-one conversations uncovered how each role experiences the same club differently.

Usability audit · Aug 2026

Can students use it?

With no live users yet, a structured walkthrough of every flow with a test club was the fastest, cheapest way to catch problems before real students did.

Recruiting participants

Who

Current e-board members: presidents, vice presidents, secretaries, chairs and outreach leads, across cultural, academic, arts and class-government organizations.

How

Distributed the survey through campus club networks and contacts at other schools. The last question asked whether leaders wanted to hear about the beta, and 8 of 13 opted in, which gave us a warm pool for follow-up conversations.

Reach

30+ e-board members across 10 universities, including Boston University, Cornell, Emory, Lehigh, Wellesley and Yale, with 10 in-depth interviews.

Privacy

Responses included names and contact details, so I report only aggregates here, and quotes are attributed by role, never by name or club.

Artifact · Survey instrument (excerpt)Google Forms · 18 questions
  1. When you became a club leader, did you receive training or information about historical activities you would now be in charge of?
  2. On a scale of 1–10, do you feel like you have to reset each year with respect to re-organizing and setting an agenda?
  3. Does your organization have an effective method for managing member contact information?
  4. Do you face any challenges promoting events to outside constituencies?
  5. Do you collaborate with other organizations in your school or neighboring schools to host joint events?
  6. If you could request one feature to make your life easier as a club officer, what would it be?

The open-ended last question produced the most useful answers, which is why the interviews that followed were built around it.

Synthesis

Define

Each role in a club hits a different pain point

Sorting what we heard by role made one thing clear: “club management” isn’t one problem. A new president and a first-year member need very different things, so each became its own design target.

RolePain pointWhat Voce gives them
New presidentOverwhelmed by logistics and communicationOne dashboard for day-to-day operations
SecretaryNo structure for agendasBuilt-in templates and task tracking
Incoming treasurerNo record of past spending or duesArchived records for a clean handoff
First-year memberUnsure how to get involvedNotifications and interest-based discovery

Three decisions that shaped the first version

Problem: too many features

Trying to fix every pain point at once, the tools list grew past 15 ideas, from budgets to virtual meetings.

What we changed

15+ ideas → 3 tools

Club profile, member directory and event calendar: the three that addressed the handoff problem most directly.

Problem: who can change what

In early versions, any member could accidentally delete a club’s files.

What we changed

Tagged every feature by who can see it. Only the e-board gets edit rights.

Problem: from design to working code

The designs lived in Figma, and a separate engineering team had to build them.

What we changed

Documented every user flow and logged issues in a format engineers could act on directly.

Phase 2 · Summer 2026

Evaluate

Using the product the way a real student would

Once the functionality was built, I set up a test club, walked through every flow as a member and as an officer, and documented anything that felt off with annotated screenshots and a specific recommendation for each.

More from the audit

Navigation

Clicking your username opened Home, but the label suggested a personal dashboard.

Rename the link “Home.”

Collab Hub

“Collab Recommend” was unclear.

Rename it “Recommendations.”

Club Files

No way to delete an accidental upload.

Add a delete option for file owners and the e-board.

Landing page

The main photo looked AI-generated, which could make students distrust the product.

Use real club logos instead of stock photos.

None of these changes is dramatic on its own. Together they decide whether a product feels intuitive or frustrating.

What I’d do next

The question that started Voce is whether a club’s knowledge survives a change in leadership. Spring e-board elections are the real test. I’d run moderated sessions with pilot clubs before and after their transition, and track whether incoming officers can find last year’s files, contacts and event history without asking anyone. Task success on that handoff is the metric I’d want the product judged by.

Retrospective

Learned

Functionality isn’t usability

A working feature only matters when people can understand and navigate it.

Small words carry big weight

Most of my audit findings were labels and counts, not broken code.

What I’d do differently

What happened

8 of the 13 survey responses came from Wellesley, where our network was strongest.

Next time

Set a quota per school before launching the survey, so one campus can’t dominate the picture.

What happened

Several survey questions were yes/no, which told us a problem existed but not how bad it was.

Next time

Pair each yes/no with a frequency or severity scale, and pilot the survey with two officers first.

What happened

The audit was my own expert walkthrough, without real officers completing tasks.

Next time

Add task-based usability tests with 5 officers and measure task completion, so findings come with numbers.

Case study 02 · Snap’N’Go · Spring 2025

When do students actually do the task?

Snap’N’Go asks students to complete quick, location-based tasks on campus, like photographing a room, through a Slack bot. Many tasks went undone. Our team analyzed the pilot’s behavioral logs to find out when and where students really participate.

My role

Co-author and poster presenter; data cleaning, analysis and visualization

Team

Four student researchers, Wellesley CS Department

Timeline

Spring 2025

Methods

Behavioral log analysis, data cleaning, heatmaps, linear regression

22,429raw task records cleaned and standardized
177accepted, completed tasks with usable data
11 AM–3 PMpeak window for task completion
Ruhlman ’25poster presented at Wellesley’s research conference

The problem

Context

A crowdsensing platform that people don’t always use

Mobile crowdsensing lets ordinary phones collect data that would otherwise need expensive sensor networks. Snap’N’Go runs as a Slack bot: it assigns a task like “take a photo of Office W311 in the Science Center,” the student accepts, completes it and earns a small reward. Every step is timestamped.

The pilot showed the same problem other crowdsensing platforms have: tasks often go uncompleted, and data quality varies. To fix that, the team needed to understand participation, not just count it.

Research questions and hypotheses

  1. Where? H1, routine alignment: students complete more tasks in places they already pass through, so completions will cluster in spatial hotspots.
  2. When? H2, time hotspots: completion depends on time of day, with more activity in the morning and early afternoon when students move around campus.

Approach

Methods

Why behavioral data

What people do

Asking students when they’d do a task invites guesses. The logs already recorded what they actually did, with no recall bias.

Why heatmaps

See the pattern first

Time-by-location heatmaps showed the shape of participation at a glance and made the findings easy to present on a poster.

Why regression

Then test it

A regression of completion on time of day checked whether the pattern in the heatmap held up as a relationship.

Process

Cleaning

Most of the work was making the data trustworthy

The raw export mixed unassigned tasks, missing timestamps and inconsistent room labels. Each cleaning decision changed which questions we could answer, so I documented every one.

22,429 task entries

Raw pilot export: assignment and acceptance times, deadlines, room numbers, anonymized user IDs.

Accepted tasks only

Dropped tasks nobody accepted, to focus on real engagement. Derived a task_completed flag from the completion timestamp.

Valid time and place

Removed tasks missing timestamps or location. Standardized room labels (“W42” vs “W420”), grouped rooms by floor, and excluded a room that doesn’t exist in the building.

177 tasks

Accepted and completed tasks with clean time and location data, plus normalized reward amounts.

Intermediate bar widths are illustrative; the start and end counts are exact.

Results

Findings

Students complete tasks in the middle of their day

Completion peaked between 11 AM and 3 PM and fell off through the evening. The regression showed the same thing: later in the day, tasks were less likely to be completed. This supported H2.

Peak completion window, 6 AM to midnight

6 AM9 AM12 PM3 PM6 PM9 PM

Where students completed tasks mattered too. The third floor had the most completed tasks, consistent with H1: students did tasks along the routes they already travel, near their classes and labs.

Recommendations for the platform

Timing

Release tasks late morning and set deadlines within the same afternoon window, instead of evenings.

Placement

Put tasks along high-traffic routes and floors students already visit, rather than spreading them evenly.

Instrumentation

Open tasks didn’t record a user ID, so we couldn’t follow individual students over time.

Fix

Log an anonymized ID for every task, so future studies can look at repeat participation and drop-off.

Retrospective

Learned

Cleaning decides the questions

The missing user IDs meant a whole line of analysis was off the table before we started. Now I check what the data can answer before I promise findings.

Logs show what, not why

We could see students skipping evening tasks, but not their reasons.

What I’d do differently

What happened

Only 177 of 22,429 records were usable, which limits how far the results generalize.

Next time

Work with the platform team to fix logging first, then run a longer pilot.

What happened

We ran a linear regression on a yes/no outcome.

Next time

Use logistic regression, which fits a completed/not-completed outcome, and report effect sizes clearly.

What happened

We never heard from the students themselves.

Next time

Add five-minute intercept interviews in the building, to explain the patterns the logs reveal.

Case study 03 · Developmental psychology · Fall 2026

Do fantastical stories change how preschoolers share?

An experiment testing whether the context of a story, fantastical or realistic, affects young children’s prosocial behavior. It’s a psychology study, but the core question is a product question: how does the way content is framed change what people do next?

In progress · analysis and write-up

My role

Student researcher; study design, sessions, analysis and APA paper

Participants

29 children, ages 3–5

Timeline

Fall 2026

Methods

Between-subjects experiment, behavioral measure, statistical analysis

Why it matters

Context

Stories are one of the main ways we teach kids to be kind

Picture books, apps and shows for preschoolers often teach sharing through talking animals and magical worlds. But do children apply lessons from a fantastical world to their own behavior as readily as lessons from a realistic one?

For anyone building learning products for kids, the answer shapes design choices: whether the setting of a lesson helps it transfer to real life.

Approach

Design

1 · Warm-up

Build comfort

A short, friendly introduction so the child is at ease with the researcher before anything is measured.

→

2 · Story

One of two conditions

Fantastical contextRealistic context

The same sharing lesson, told in a different setting.

→

3 · Sharing task

Measure behavior

The child gets the chance to share with someone else. How much they share is the outcome.

Why an experiment

To show cause

Comparing two otherwise identical stories isolates the effect of context. Observation alone couldn’t separate it from everything else in a child’s life.

Why a behavioral measure

Kids show, not tell

Three-year-olds can’t reliably report what they would do. Watching what they actually share is more valid than asking.

Why between-subjects

No carry-over

Each child hears one story, so the first story can’t shape how they respond to the second.

Operations

Running it

Research with three-year-olds takes extra care

Consent

Parents give informed consent; each child also gives verbal assent and can stop at any time.

Consistency

A scripted procedure keeps every session the same, so differences come from the story condition, not from how I ran it.

Adapting

Short sessions, simple wording and patience with wandering attention, without changing the parts of the script that matter for the comparison.

Privacy

Data is de-identified and stored by participant number, never by name.

What this taught me about usability testing

Preschoolers don’t hide confusion and don’t fake interest. Running these sessions sharpened the skills I use with adult users: a neutral script, a warm rapport, and noticing what people do instead of what they say.
